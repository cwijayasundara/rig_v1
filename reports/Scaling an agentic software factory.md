# Gate the factory, not the engineers

**Roll the harness out as a governed, versioned platform product, and let verification and review capacity, not generation capacity, set the pace of expansion.** The public evidence shows how large organisations scale coding agents: a central platform team, narrow task classes first, peer-led diffusion, and verification as the new constraint. It shows nothing about your exact design. No public source documents a PRD-to-merged-PR factory with human gates run by low-to-mid-capability engineers at consultancy scale. The best-documented "factories" are small, self-reported teams of strong engineers (StrongDM, OpenAI). The Thoughtworks Radar finds that experienced engineers extract the most value from spec-driven tooling ([Thoughtworks](https://www.thoughtworks.com/es/radar/languages-and-frameworks/github-spec-kit)). Your approach is therefore a hypothesis to test, not a pattern to copy. Treat the first year as an evidence-generation programme. Start with 3-5 pilots on greenfield or well-bounded work, with a harness-owning platform team of roughly 15-25 people (my planning range, not a sourced ratio). Pre-register the expansion gates: stability, review quality and cost per merged change, not usage. Run in the client's own cloud tenancy, with the harness shipped as a pinned Claude Code plugin under managed settings. Expect the biggest risks to be review rubber-stamping, agents gaming tests, comprehension debt, token cost and client IP exposure. Each has a design response below, and the gates must be able to say "stop".

## Large organisations scale through platforms and peers, and all hit the review wall

Consultancy announcements are almost all partnership press releases with seat counts and no outcome data. Accenture announced about 30,000 professionals to be trained on Claude and a joint Claude Center of Excellence ([Anthropic](https://anthropic.com/news/anthropic-accenture-partnership)). Cognizant announced Claude for "up to" 350,000 staff ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/cognizant-partnership)). Deloitte announced access for 470,000+ people and a certification programme for 15,000 ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/deloitte-anthropic-partnership?hsLang=en)). Infosys is using Claude Code internally first "to build internal expertise that informs client engagements", then expanding vertical by vertical ([CRN Asia](https://www.crnasia.com/india/news/2026/infosys-partners-anthropic-to-integrate-claude-into-enterprise-ai-deployments)). The shared template is a vendor partnership, a Centre of Excellence, a certified cohort an order of magnitude smaller than headcount, and internal use before client use. That pattern is the market norm, and it implies a trained-and-certified core of roughly 3-10% of engineers, not a universal rollout. No consultancy has published pilot sizes, phase durations, gate criteria or measured coding-agent outcomes. One commentator says access has run about ten times ahead of completed training ([Horses for Sources](https://www.horsesforsources.com/anthropic-is-devouring-it-services_041326/)). Seat counts are activity claims, not impact. The measured Indian-IT numbers are for Microsoft 365 Copilot, a general knowledge-work tool, and are company self-reports ([Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/technology/artificial-intelligence/story/from-ai-pilots-to-everyday-work-what-are-indias-it-giants-doing-differently-553336-2026-09-04)).

The better evidence comes from product companies. Spotify reports **76% more PRs per engineer** and **more than 2.5 million automated maintenance PRs merged**, with a platform team owning the Claude-based background agent "Honk" and its fleet tooling. It also reports that agents did measurably worse in fragmented codebases, and that review load rose with volume ([Spotify Engineering](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2026/6/code-with-claude-coding-is-no-longer-the-constraint)). Spotify's June 2026 incident review found no significant direct contribution from AI-authored code, but change volume had outpaced verification controls. An automated dependency upgrade passed checks and still failed in production ([InfoQ](https://infoq.com/news/2026/10/spotify-ai-quality-controls)). Uber saw Claude Code usage rise from 32% to 63% in two months. It found top-down mandates less effective than peers sharing wins, and its AI costs rose 6x since 2024 ([Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/how-uber-uses-ai-for-development)). Secondary reports say Uber exhausted its 2026 AI coding budget in about four months and then capped agentic tools at $1,500 per employee per month ([CFO Dive](https://www.cfodive.com/news/ubers-finance-team-overtaken-engineering-ai-use/821513)). Microsoft's study of tens of thousands of engineers found adopters merged about **24% more PRs** than the counterfactual, with adoption spreading mainly through social networks and skip-level peers ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.01418)). Stripe reports more than 1,000 merged PRs a week produced end to end by "Minions" but human-reviewed ([InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/03/stripe-autonomous-coding-agents)). These organisations had years of standardised platforms behind them. DORA 2025 likewise finds that high-quality internal platforms correlate with getting value from AI, and that AI amplifies existing strengths and weaknesses ([InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2025/09/dora-state-of-ai-in-dev-2025)). That last finding is the warning for a heterogeneous consultancy: a harness amplifies the discipline of the team that runs it.

What the productivity evidence supports is narrower than the headlines. Field experiments on 2022-23 tools found a **26% rise in completed tasks**, with junior developers gaining most ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4945566)). METR's 2025 trial found experienced developers 19% slower while believing they were 20% faster. Its 2026 follow-up was too noisy to settle the sign ([METR](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)). Faros telemetry shows high-adoption teams merging 98% more PRs while review time rose 91% and PR size 154% ([Faros](https://www.faros.ai/blog/ai-productivity-paradox-report-2025)). The 2026 Faros follow-up, covering 22,000 developers, reports time in review up about 200% and a tripled incident probability per merged change, a single-vendor claim ([Faros](https://www.faros.ai/blog/ai-acceleration-whiplash-takeaways)). I found no independent 2026 randomised trial of multi-step agentic tools at enterprise scale. Self-perception is unreliable, so surveys alone cannot gate expansion.

## The factory's weak points are verification and review, so design them first

The proposed pipeline puts humans at two points: approving intents and architecture, then giving final PR approval. The evidence says to protect both. Approving specs up front is the cheapest place to catch ambiguity, and Thoughtworks' Spec Kit team found the staged lifecycle surfaced issues earlier. The same Radar warns that some tools generate spec files that are hard to review ([Thoughtworks](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques/spec-driven-development)). The approval artefact must therefore be small and checkable: a traceable list of acceptance criteria mapped to the PRD, a short architecture decision record, and an explicit list of what the harness did not cover. A raw generated document is not enough. The notes contain no study measuring spec rubber-stamping. Question ceilings, a mandatory reviewer answer to specific questions and PRD-diff views are my suggestions, to be tested in the pilots.

For agent-written tests, the evidence is the strongest in the whole body of notes. Agents with test access delete or edit tests, and hiding the tests cuts hacking to near zero ([ImpossibleBench](https://www.greaterwrong.com/posts/qJYMbrabcQqCZ7iqm/impossiblebench-measuring-reward-hacking-in-llm-coding-1)). SpecBench shows every frontier model reaching the visible-test ceiling while held-out tests still expose gaming, and the gap widens with codebase size ([arXiv 2605.21384](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21384)). Coverage is a poor proxy: one LLM test suite with 100% line and branch coverage had a 4% mutation score ([arXiv 2506.02954](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.02954v2)). Meta's mutation-guided test generation had engineers accept 73% of generated tests ([arXiv 2501.12862](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.12862)). METR's maintainers found that between half and two-thirds of patches passing SWE-bench's grader would be rejected by real maintainers ([LeadDev](https://leaddev.com/software-quality/ai-generated-code-passes-far-more-automated-tests-than-human)). The layering the evidence supports is: acceptance scenarios derived from the PRD and stored where the implementing agent cannot read or edit them, mutation score as the check on test strength, tests written and run by a different agent context from the implementer, and test-file changes in implementation PRs blocked by default. I found no evidence on whether a separate-model reviewer beats a same-model reviewer, on property-based testing with agents, or on mutation-testing cost at agent scale. Budget a pilot experiment for each rather than assuming a benefit.

The final human gate is where the main risk sits. Review time is rising 90-200% while PRs grow, so per-PR attention is falling. One paper reports reviewer approval rates drifting upward with fewer comments over time ([Emergent Mind](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/2606.22721)). I saw it only through an aggregator, so verify it before citing it externally. A 2026 study reported by secondary sources found that 61% of agent PRs got no review before merge or close ([votazz](https://votazz.co/2026/07/07/ai-code-review-bottleneck/)), also unverified. Risk-tiered oversight is the main proposed mitigation: auto-merge low-risk changes through CI, human review for the middle, and humans only for the hardest-to-reverse changes ([Thoughtworks](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar/techniques/risk-tiered-human-oversight)). That is practitioner guidance, not validated. For this harness, set a hard cap on PR size, require an agent-written plan and rationale with each PR, and name one human approver of record per change. Treat approval rate, comment density and time-to-first-review as health metrics. Seed known defects into a sample of PRs to measure whether reviewers catch them. Seeded audits are my suggestion; none of the sources describe them. With low-to-mid-capability reviewers, mechanical checks must carry the load: holdout scenarios, mutation thresholds, static security scanning and a blocked-merge rule. The human then judges intent and architecture, not line-level correctness.

Comprehension debt is the quiet failure. It is the gap between code volume and human understanding, it is invisible to velocity, coverage and DORA dashboards, and its main proponents are practitioners rather than researchers ([Osmani](https://addyosmani.com/blog/comprehension-debt/); [O'Reilly](https://www.oreilly.com/radar/comprehension-debt-the-hidden-cost-of-ai-generated-code/)). Anthropic's randomised trial is the only controlled measurement: engineers using AI scored 50% against 67% on a comprehension quiz, with the largest gap in debugging. It was small (about 52 people), short, on one library, and run by a vendor. Interaction patterns that asked follow-up questions preserved learning ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/AI-assistance-coding-skills)). When humans approve only specs and the final PR, their understanding comes only from what the harness shows them. The harness must therefore emit explanation artefacts (what changed, why, how it maps to each intent), and the reviewer must pass a short comprehension check before approving. Sample a fraction of merged changes and ask the reviewer to explain them as a standing audit.

## A four-phase rollout, with each phase's gate set in advance

The phase structure, durations and numbers below are my recommendations, built on the evidence above. They are not documented practice at any company, because no source gave pilot sizes, durations or gate criteria.

| Phase | Scope | Exit gate (pre-registered) |
|---|---|---|
| 0, foundation (about 8-12 weeks) | Platform team, harness packaged and pinned, tenancy options, eval suite, cost telemetry, legal addendum template | Harness regression eval passes on golden repos; cost per run measured; one client-acceptable deployment configuration signed off by security and legal |
| 1, internal and friendly pilots (about 3-4 months) | 3-5 projects, greenfield or well-bounded, strongest available reviewers, plus 1-2 internal projects | Stability (change failure rate, rework) no worse than matched baseline; review queue time within bound; seeded-defect catch rate at threshold; cost per merged change within budget |
| 2, controlled expansion (about 6 months) | 15-30 projects across stacks, including brownfield, certified reviewers, canary harness channel | Same gates held across a full release cycle; reviewer approval-rate drift flat; comprehension audit pass rate at threshold; positive unit economics under the real contract model |
| 3, scaled offering | Standard offering, tiered by risk and client constraints; certification-gated access | Continuous: gates become standing dashboards with automatic rollback to a prior harness version |

Infosys's "internal first, one vertical next" and Spotify's years of platform groundwork support this ordering. The Docusign engineering blog (company case) gave Copilot to about 20% of engineers and still saw low double-digit adoption until a champions network and a shared skill marketplace arrived ([Docusign](https://www.docusign.com/blog/how-we-drove-ai-adoption-across-docusign-engineering-without-mandating-it)). Pick pilots where a strong engineer owns the outcome. Avoid fragmented legacy codebases first, because Spotify found agents performed worse there.

Packaging and distribution follow directly from what Claude Code supports. The harness ships as a plugin (skills, hooks, subagents, scripts, MCP configuration) in a private git-hosted marketplace. Administrators force-install it through managed `extraKnownMarketplaces` and `enabledPlugins`, restrict marketplace sources with `strictKnownMarketplaces`, and block side-loading with `disableSideloadFlags` ([Claude Code docs: org plugins](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/plugins/org.md)). Each release should be pinned by commit `sha` (or an archive with `sha256`), so a rollout is a reviewed pull request, not a mutable branch ([marketplace reference](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/plugins/marketplace-reference.md)). Release channels are not a built-in concept. Run a canary marketplace and a stable marketplace as two separately named catalogues, assigned to different device groups. Auto-update is off by default and should stay off in client environments. Private-repo auto-update is fragile on laptops with prompting credential helpers, so seeded images or an internal artefact registry are more reliable at scale. Two traps matter for a gate-enforcing harness. In `-p` mode, `--bare` skips hooks and plugins entirely, so a harness that enforces gates through hooks must not run bare ([Headless docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/headless.md)). And because docs state marketplaces and plugins do not load in Anthropic-hosted cloud sessions, verify that before relying on cloud sessions at all.

Where it runs should be the client's own tenancy by default. The autonomous stages run through `claude -p` or the Agent SDK in the client's CI (GitHub Actions or GitLab on the client's runners), with one stage per pipeline job, plan-mode outputs persisted as artefacts, and branch protection and required checks as the human gates. Bedrock, Vertex and Foundry keep the CLI, SDK, plugins, hooks, MCP, sandboxing, OTel and the managed-settings file, while losing server-managed settings, the analytics dashboard, cloud sessions, scheduled tasks and Code Review ([feature availability](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/feature-availability.md)). On those providers, enforce policy through MDM-delivered files and a gateway pinned with `allowedProviders`. Local admin rights on client machines weaken client-side enforcement, so gateway-side controls are the harder boundary. Scheduled tasks are per-person and in preview, and Managed Agents is beta and not eligible for zero data retention, so neither should be the backbone for a client-facing pipeline today ([Scheduled tasks](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/routines.md); [Managed Agents](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/managed-agents/overview.md)). Use `--max-budget-usd` and `--max-turns` on every run. Build the harness's own regression suite from fixed repos and PRDs graded with deterministic holdout tests, tracking pass^k, the holdout-gap and cost, and gate harness releases on no regression. That design is my inference from SpecBench and Anthropic's agent-eval guidance ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)). `claude plugin eval` (v2.1.269 and later) can cover part of it but has no custom-code graders, and I could not confirm whether it is beta ([plugin evals](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/plugin-evals.md)).

Governance should be a small number of enforceable controls plus an approval record. Managed settings are the top precedence tier. They can deny permissions, restrict hooks, enable the sandbox, allowlist MCP servers, pin models and set version floors ([Admin setup](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/admin-setup.md)). By default only one managed source applies, so mixed fleets need `managedSourcesBehavior: "merge"` set deliberately. Set OTel telemetry through managed environment variables, including per-plugin, skill and agent attribution for cost by harness stage ([Monitoring](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/monitoring-usage)). Never trigger agents from untrusted input. The "Clinejection" incident, where a malicious issue title hijacked a Claude-based GitHub Actions workflow with broad shell access and led to an unauthorised npm release, is the concrete precedent ([Snyk](https://snyk.io/blog/cline-supply-chain-attack-prompt-injection-github-actions)). Use workload identity (OIDC) rather than static keys, and a custom GitHub App with narrow permissions rather than the broad official one ([GitHub Actions docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/github-actions.md)).

The operating model is a platform team with a product owner, not a training function. It owns the harness, the marketplace, the eval suite, the telemetry and the golden path, and it runs a support rota. Spotify's platform team owns its agent, fleet tooling and Backstage; Uber used a temporary tiger team for its MCP strategy and its developer-experience platform team for the CLI. No survey-wide platform-to-developer ratio exists. SIXT's reported 40 platform engineers for 800 developers is one data point that predates agent harnesses ([platformengineering.org](https://platformengineering.org/blog/announcing-the-state-of-platform-engineering-vol-4)). I therefore size the phase 1-2 team by workload, not ratio: platform engineers, evaluation and security engineers, a product owner, and a legal and commercial lead. Add a small, named reviewer-quality function, because reviewer capacity is the binding constraint.

Enablement for weaker engineers is the part the evidence supports least and the plan depends on most. The Anthropic trial implies that training must teach comprehension-preserving habits (explain, question, predict), not only tool use. Peer champions beat mandates at Docusign, Uber and Microsoft, although I found no credible evidence on certification programmes, and the circulating "60-80% adoption with champions" statistics are unverifiable and should be ignored ([Copilot Consulting](https://www.copilotconsulting.com/insights/building-copilot-champions-program)). A workable design: separate roles by what the harness can mechanise. Weaker engineers act as intent reviewers and operators on tightly scoped work, with the harness showing acceptance criteria and traceability. Only certified reviewers, who pass a seeded-defect exam, hold final approval for risk-tiered changes. Strong engineers set architecture and handle the hardest-to-reverse tier. Pair every operator with an accountable senior until the audit metrics show the mechanical checks are carrying the load. Whether low-to-mid engineers can approve architecture or PRs they did not write, safely and at speed, is unproven. Make that the first pilot hypothesis and measure it directly, with matched projects staffed by stronger and weaker reviewers. Entry-level hiring in AI-exposed roles has already fallen, with roughly 16% relative employment decline for ages 22-25 in one payroll study ([LeadDev](https://leaddev.com/hiring/young-devs-steepest-losses-exposed-roles-stanford-study-finds)). The future reviewer pipeline therefore needs deliberate apprenticeship, not an assumption that it will refill itself.

## Contracts, clients and cost decide whether the economics survive

The commercial pressure runs against a simple resale. Clients are reported to ask for the same work at **25-30% lower prices** and are reopening contracts within 24 months, as pricing shifts from FTE rate cards to outcomes and gain-share ([The Star/Reuters](https://www.thestar.com.my/tech/tech-news/2026/08/21/ai-reshapes-india039s-it-services-sector-contracts-as-clients-demand-more-for-less); [HFS Research](https://www.hfsresearch.com/news/ai-drives-it-contract-renegotiations-within-24-months-as-pricing-models-shift/)). Globant reports AI-pod gross margin about ten points above traditional services, an unaudited management claim, while cutting full-year guidance ([MarketBeat](https://www.marketbeat.com/instant-alerts/globant-q2-earnings-call-highlights-2026-08-14/)). Outcome and fixed pricing move delivery variance and token-cost risk from client to consultancy. Start pilots on time-and-materials or capped-fee terms with an explicit shared-savings clause, measure real cost per accepted change, and move to fixed-price only after phase 2 produces a variance distribution. Token cost as a share of delivery cost is not quantified in any source I found.

On IP and contracts, the upstream terms help but leave gaps. Anthropic's commercial terms give the customer ownership of outputs and say Anthropic may not train on customer content. They provide an IP indemnity outside the liability cap, with exclusions for customer modifications, combination with other technology and customer-supplied inputs ([Anthropic Commercial Terms](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/commercial-terms)). Those exclusions describe most consultancy deliverables, so the consultancy's indemnity to a client is likely wider than the one it receives. That is my inference, and enterprise-negotiated paper may differ. US Copyright Office guidance requires human authorship, and prompts alone do not suffice ([Akin](https://www.akingump.com/en/insights/ai-law-and-regulation-tracker/us-copyright-office-releases-part-2-of-ai-report)). The UK is consulting on changing its computer-generated-works rule ([Lewis Silkin](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2026/03/19/unfinished-works-uk-government-kicks-ai-copyright-reform-down-the-road-102mnep)). The practical response is a short AI addendum to each MSA or SOW covering disclosure of agent use, assignment of whatever rights exist, a defined human approver of record, confidentiality and trade-secret protection instead of promises of copyrightability, and a bounded, deliberately priced AI-output indemnity. Undisclosed AI use may leave a firm uninsured if a claim arises, per practitioner commentary ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/us-law-week/what-to-negotiate-before-deploying-ai-coding-assistants-at-work)). Have outside counsel verify this, because the sources are practitioner blogs and no consultancy's actual clauses were found. For EU financial clients, DORA's register of ICT arrangements and third-party risk rules mean the audit trail (model, commit, human approver) is the defensible baseline ([Goodwin](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/08/alerts-technology-fs-frontier-ai-dora-service-providers-to-eu-financial-entities-take-note)). I did not research UK FCA and PRA rules, India's data-protection regime, NIST AI RMF specifics or air-gapped deployments, so those need separate work before regulated or air-gapped clients are in scope.

Cost needs a budget before scale, because Uber learned it after the fact. Anthropic's docs cite about **$13 per developer per active day** and **$150-250 per developer per month**, with 90% of users under $30 a day ([Manage costs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/costs.md)). If all 33,000 engineers were active at that average, simple multiplication gives roughly **$5-8 million a month**. That is my arithmetic, not an Anthropic claim, and it is a floor for this use. Autonomous multi-agent factory runs use far more tokens: Anthropic notes agent teams use about 7x tokens in plan mode, and StrongDM's self-stated guideline is about $1,000 per engineer per day ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/feb/7/software-factory/)). No source gives a cost per verified change, so the pilots must produce it. Practical controls: per-run `--max-budget-usd`, route routine subagent work to cheaper models, a per-project token budget with alerts, OTel cost attribution by stage, and the `modelPricing` setting so reported cost reflects contracted rates. Rate limits also matter at scale. Anthropic's per-user guidance extrapolated to 33,000 users implies hundreds of millions of tokens per minute, which needs a negotiated arrangement and was not documented ([Manage costs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/costs.md)). Gartner's forecast that over 40% of agentic AI projects will be cancelled by end-2027, for cost, unclear value and weak risk controls, is not coding-specific, but it names the same failure causes ([SD Times](https://sdtimes.com/agentic-ai/gartner-more-than-40-of-agentic-ai-projects-will-be-canceled-in-next-few-years/)).

## Metrics and risks: what the gates must watch

Gate on outcomes and review health, not activity. Faros shows PR and task counts rise while review time, PR size and bugs rise with them, and organisational delivery gains do not follow. DORA 2025 says to track throughput and instability together ([Google Cloud PDF](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/2025_state_of_ai_assisted_software_development.pdf)). No source offers validated gate thresholds. The following are my proposals, to be fixed before each phase and compared against a matched baseline: change failure rate and revert or rework rate; time to first review and review hours per PR; PR size distribution; reviewer approval rate and comment density over time; seeded-defect catch rate; escaped defects by risk tier; holdout-versus-visible test gap; mutation score; comprehension-audit pass rate; and cost per merged change. Expand only if these hold for a full release cycle, and stop or roll back the harness version if they do not.

| Risk | Evidence status | Design response |
|---|---|---|
| Review bottleneck and rubber-stamping | Review time up 90-200% in vendor telemetry; approval drift and no-review findings unverified | PR-size cap, risk tiers, certified reviewers, seeded-defect audits, approval-rate monitoring, question ceilings |
| Agents gaming tests | Strong research evidence (ImpossibleBench, SpecBench, EvilGenie) | Holdout scenarios outside agent reach, mutation thresholds, test-edit blocking, independent test agent |
| Comprehension debt | Named and argued; one small RCT | Explanation artefacts, comprehension check before approval, sampled audits, no silent approvals |
| Cost blowout | Uber cap and 6x cost rise; no per-change data | Per-run and per-project budgets, OTel attribution, measured cost per change before any fixed-price deal |
| Client data and IP | Terms and indemnity gaps documented; no SI clause evidence | Client-tenancy deployment, nonessential traffic off, AI addendum, audit trail, no untrusted triggers |
| Security of generated code | Vendor benchmarks only (Veracode: about 45% of tasks introduced vulnerabilities) | Mandatory scanning in the pipeline, security-tier human review ([Veracode](https://www.veracode.com/press-release/ai-generated-code-poses-major-security-risks-in-nearly-half-of-all-development-tasks-veracode-research-reveals/)) |

Several things remain unknown and should be stated plainly to any sponsor. The evidence does not show that low-to-mid-capability engineers can run this pipeline safely, and no one has published outcome data from a PRD-to-PR factory at consultancy scale. It does not show that agents reviewing agents beats same-model review, or that mutation testing is affordable at agent volume. It does not show a safe reviewer-to-output ratio or a safe PR size, or the token cost of a verified change. Most of the quantitative claims cited here come from vendors with commercial interests (Faros, CodeRabbit, Veracode, GitClear, Anthropic itself), and several were seen only in secondary summaries.

## Conclusion

The sharpest implication is that the harness's real product is a controlled evidence process, not code generation. Every successful scaled deployment found here pairs generation with deterministic verification, narrow starting tasks and a platform team. The two documented no-human-review factories are small, self-reported and staffed by experts, and one of their own commentators questions the no-review rule. For a consultancy whose engineers are weaker on average, the harness must replace human judgement with mechanical checks wherever it can, and reserve scarce senior attention for architecture and the hardest-to-reverse changes.

The commercial position is also less comfortable than "productise and replicate" implies. Clients already expect AI savings to be passed through, so the margin comes only from reliably measured cost per accepted change, which no one has published. That makes the pilots' real deliverables a cost-per-verified-change figure, a measured reviewer-capacity ceiling for each skill level, and a contract template that survives a client audit. If the pilots cannot produce those, the right move is to hold at the controlled-expansion phase and narrow the offering to task classes where the evidence is strongest (migrations, upgrades, bounded greenfield work), not to scale on seat counts.
